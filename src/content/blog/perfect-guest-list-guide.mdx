## Mastering Your Wedding Guest List & Seating Charts

Creating your guest list is often cited as the most stressful part of wedding planning. Clear rules and strategic seating charts keep family politics at bay.

### Guest List Rules
1. **Capacity Limit**: Never exceed your venue's maximum fire capacity.
2. **Consistent Plus-One Policy**: Apply rules equally across all friend and family groups.
3. **Seating Harmony**: Mix friendly social groups while keeping conflicting relatives separated.

## How Many Guests Can You Actually Invite?

Before you write a single name, work out your real limit. Your guest count is set by the smallest of three numbers:

- **Venue capacity** for the layout you want. A room that holds 200 for a standing cocktail party may seat only 140 for a plated dinner with a dance floor.
- **Budget per head.** Divide the amount you can spend on catering, drinks and rentals by your expected per-person cost. If catering costs $85 per person and you have $10,000 for food and drink, your limit is about 115 guests before cake, staff and rentals.
- **What you want the day to feel like.** Sixty guests feels intimate, 150 feels like a party and 300 feels like an event. Decide which one you want before family suggestions start arriving.

Use the [Wedding Budget Calculator](/tools/budget-calculator) to test how different guest counts change your total.

## Step-by-Step: Building the Guest List

### Step 1: Agree on the split

Decide together how many places go to each side. Common approaches include an equal three-way split between the couple and each set of parents, or a split based on who is contributing to the budget. Agree on this early, because changing the rules later causes far more tension than setting them at the start.

### Step 2: Write the "dream list" without editing

Each person writes every name they might invite. Include partners, children and anyone you feel obliged to invite. Seeing the full list in one place shows you how far over your limit you are, and that number makes the next conversations easier.

### Step 3: Sort into tiers

Sort every name into one of three groups:

| Tier | Who belongs here | Invite timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A-list | Immediate family, closest friends, wedding party and their partners | First round, with save-the-dates |
| B-list | Extended family, colleagues, friends you see less often | Second round, as A-list declines arrive |
| C-list | People you would invite only if the venue or budget had unlimited room | Usually not invited |

### Step 4: Apply your cutting rules

When the A-list is still too long, consistent rules make cuts feel fair rather than personal. Popular rules include:

- **The two-year rule:** if neither of you has spoken to them in two years, they are not on the A-list.
- **The partner rule:** if you have never met their partner, they receive a single invitation.
- **The work rule:** invite all of your immediate team or none of it.
- **The children rule:** invite only immediate family's children, or make the whole celebration adults-only.

### Step 5: Track everything in one place

Keep names, addresses, RSVP status, meal choices, dietary needs and table numbers in a single list. The free [Guest List Manager](/tools/guest-list-manager) does this in your browser and exports a CSV you can share with your caterer.

## Running an A/B List Without Hurt Feelings

An A/B list only works if B-list guests never feel like a back-up. Follow these timing rules:

1. Send A-list invitations 10 to 12 weeks before the wedding, with an RSVP deadline about four weeks before the date.
2. As soon as a decline arrives, send the next B-list invitation the same week.
3. Stop sending B-list invitations about six to eight weeks before the wedding. Invitations that arrive later make it obvious that guests were a second choice.
4. Use the same RSVP card design for both lists, but print a later RSVP date on B-list cards so everyone has a reasonable reply window.

## Plus-One Rules That Keep the Peace

Plus-ones can add 10 to 20 percent to your guest count. Decide your rule once and use it for everyone:

- **Always invite:** spouses, fiancés, long-term partners and people who live together.
- **Usually invite:** members of the wedding party, so they have company while you are busy.
- **Consider:** single guests who will know nobody else at the wedding.
- **Usually not needed:** guests who will be sitting with a large group of friends.

Address the invitation to exactly the people invited. "Ms Priya Shah and Guest" invites a plus-one. "Ms Priya Shah" does not. Your [RSVP and invitation planning](/tools/wedding-planning-timeline) should include a polite reply ready for anyone who asks to bring someone extra.

## How to Build a Seating Chart Step by Step

Start the seating chart only when about 80 percent of RSVPs are in. Earlier versions will be rewritten many times.

1. **Draw the room.** Get the floor plan from your venue, with table sizes. Round tables usually seat 8 to 10 guests, and long banquet tables can make the room feel more social.
2. **Place the fixed tables first.** The head or sweetheart table, the parents' tables and any guests who need easy access to exits or restrooms, such as older relatives or guests with mobility needs.
3. **Group by connection.** Seat people in groups that already know each other: school friends, colleagues, cousins, neighbours.
4. **Mix with care.** Put friendly, outgoing guests at tables where people know each other less well.
5. **Separate known conflicts.** Seat divorced parents or relatives who do not get along at different tables, at a similar distance from the couple, so neither side feels less important.
6. **Think about the music.** Keep older guests a little further from the speakers and younger guests closer to the dance floor.
7. **Check meal choices.** If you serve a plated dinner, add each guest's meal to their place card or escort card so servers can work quickly.

## Handling Tricky Family Situations

- **Parents who want to invite their friends:** if they are contributing to the budget, give them a fixed number of places and let them choose within it.
- **Divorced or separated parents:** speak to each parent privately about seating and photos before the day, so nobody is surprised.
- **Relatives who were not invited to the last family wedding:** you are not obliged to follow someone else's list. Base your list on your own relationships.
- **Guests who RSVP late or not at all:** call or message them two to three days after the RSVP deadline. A friendly personal message gets a faster answer than a second card.

## Guest List and Seating Timeline

| When | Task |
| --- | --- |
| 12+ months before | Agree on guest count range and how places are split |
| Before booking the venue | Finish the A-list so you book the right room size |
| 6 to 8 months before | Collect addresses and send save-the-dates to the A-list |
| 10 to 12 weeks before | Mail A-list invitations |
| 6 to 8 weeks before | Last B-list invitations go out |
| 4 weeks before | RSVP deadline and follow-up calls |
| 2 to 3 weeks before | Final seating chart, escort cards and meal list |
| 1 to 2 weeks before | Final headcount to caterer and venue |
