## Mastering Your Wedding Guest List & Seating Charts

Creating your guest list is often cited as the most stressful part of wedding planning. Clear rules and strategic seating charts keep family politics at bay.

The guest list is also the decision that drives almost everything else: venue size, catering cost, invitation count, and even the style of the celebration. Settling it early, with a few firm rules, makes every later choice easier.

### Guest List Rules
1. **Capacity Limit**: Never exceed your venue's maximum fire capacity.
2. **Consistent Plus-One Policy**: Apply rules equally across all friend and family groups.
3. **Seating Harmony**: Mix friendly social groups while keeping conflicting relatives separated.

## Start with your guest count

How many people can you invite? Let the numbers answer, not the family tree.

1. **Check the venue's capacity.** Ask for the maximum for a seated dinner, which is usually lower than the cocktail-style number. Our [venue selection guide](/blog/how-to-choose-wedding-venue) explains what else to ask.
2. **Check the budget.** Catering, drinks, rentals, and favors are mostly charged per person, so each guest adds a real cost. Our [wedding budget tips](/blog/wedding-budget-tips) show how trimming the list is often the fastest way to lower the total.
3. **Write your target.** Pick a number you are comfortable with, plus a hard ceiling you will not pass.

## Build an A-list and B-list

What goes on the master list? Everyone you would invite if there were no limits. Then sort them into tiers.

- **A-list:** Immediate family, your closest friends, and the people you cannot imagine the day without. They get first-round invitations.
- **B-list:** Guests you would love to have if space allows, such as extended family, colleagues, or friends you see less often.

When A-list declines arrive, you invite B-list guests to fill the spaces. To make this work, send first-round invitations early enough that replacements still have time to respond, and never tell B-list guests they were a second round.

### A simple way to sort

Ask three questions about each name:

- Have we spoken or met in the last year?
- Would we be sad if they were not there?
- Would they expect an invitation, and would that matter to us?

A "yes" to the first two is an A-list. A "yes" to only one is a B-list. A "no" to all three can usually come off.

You can keep every name, tier, and RSVP in one place with our [guest list manager](/tools/guest-list-manager), or follow the steps in the [wedding guest list organizer checklist](/checklists/wedding-guest-list-organizer).

## Set plus-one and kids rules

Plus-one and children rules cause more hurt feelings than almost any other policy, mostly when they are applied unevenly. Decide once, then apply it to everyone.

### Plus-one rules that stay fair

A clear rule is easier to explain than a list of exceptions. Common versions:

- Guests who are married, engaged, or living together always receive a plus-one.
- Members of the wedding party receive a plus-one.
- Other single guests receive a plus-one only if space allows, or not at all.

Address invitations by name. An envelope that lists only the guest's name tells them, without a conversation, that the invitation is for them alone.

### Kids at the wedding

Decide whether children are invited and say so plainly. If you choose an adults-only reception, put it on the invitation or enclosure card and address envelopes to named adults. If children are invited, consider a kids' table or an activity bag, and check the venue's rules and the extra cost of children's meals.

## Handle family dynamics

How do you handle a parent who wants to invite thirty extra people? Treat it as a numbers conversation, not an emotional one.

- **Give each side a share.** If your families are contributing, offer each parent a number of invitations. They fill their share however they like.
- **Set the rule before the requests.** It is easier to say "we have a fixed number" in advance than to refuse a request after the list is full.
- **Say no kindly and briefly.** "We are keeping this wedding small and could not include everyone we love" is true, kind, and does not invite negotiation.
- **Keep a short backup list.** If someone declines, your B-list has an answer ready.

When tension builds, remember that the list belongs to the couple. Listening is generous. Agreeing to every request is not required.

## Plan the seating chart

When should you start? After most RSVPs arrive, so you are not rearranging tables for guests who are not coming.

### Step by step

1. **Get your venue's table layout and sizes.** Round tables, long tables, and head tables seat different numbers, and the room may limit your choices.
2. **Group guests into natural clusters.** Family, college friends, work friends, and neighbors usually belong together. Our [guest list organizer](/checklists/wedding-guest-list-organizer) helps you tag these groups.
3. **Seat the key tables first.** Place the couple's table, parents, grandparents, and the wedding party before everyone else.
4. **Fill in the rest by connection.** Seat people near others they know, and mix in a few outgoing guests to help shyer ones.
5. **Review for conflicts.** Look for guests who should not sit near each other and for anyone who might feel isolated.
6. **Add accessibility needs.** Seat older guests, wheelchair users, and parents of young children near exits, restrooms, and quiet corners.
7. **Check against the headcount.** Confirm every table total with your caterer's final number.

### Seating tips that save headaches

- Seat singles together only if they would enjoy it. A "singles table" can feel awkward for some.
- Place guests with limited mobility away from speakers and the dance floor if they prefer quiet.
- Print a table number and a name for each guest. A seating display or escort cards makes arrival easier.
- Keep a small spare chart for late changes, since no-shows and last-minute additions are common.

## Guest list and seating checklist

- Set maximum guest count from venue capacity and budget
- Write the master list and sort A-list and B-list
- Decide plus-one and kids rules, then apply them to everyone
- Give each family a number of invitations
- Send A-list invitations early, then B-list as declines arrive
- Collect RSVPs and meal choices
- Draft the seating chart after most RSVPs are in
- Finalize the chart after the venue's headcount deadline
